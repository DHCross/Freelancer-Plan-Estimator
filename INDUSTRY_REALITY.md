# Why Freelance Creative Work Remains a Side Gig

## The Core Problem

In creative industries like RPG publishing, freelance work is structurally unsustainable as primary income for most contractors. This is not due to lack of talent or effort—it is a direct consequence of how projects are managed and how risk is distributed.

## The Vision Problem

### Management Owns the Vision, Contractors Bear the Cost

When a project's creative direction remains fluid during production:

1. **Scope is undefined** - "The book" is not a fixed deliverable but an evolving target
2. **Completion is unmeasurable** - "Done" cannot be defined when revisions continue during production
3. **Timeline is fiction** - Deadlines assume stable scope, which does not exist
4. **Budget is meaningless** - Fixed quotes cannot price variable work

### The Typical Pattern

1. Project begins with initial scope
2. Creative lead has additional ideas during production
3. "Small additions" accumulate into significant rework
4. Contractor absorbs unpaid labor to keep project moving
5. Management perceives delays as contractor failure
6. Contractor is blamed or penalized for timeline impact

## Per-Project Pricing: A Structural Trap

### When Flat-Rate Works

Fixed-price contracts are viable when:
- Manuscript is locked before production begins
- All assets (art, text, data) are finalized and delivered
- Revision rounds are contractually limited
- Approval criteria are objective and documented
- Scope changes trigger contract amendments with additional payment

### When Flat-Rate Fails

Fixed-price contracts become exploitation when:
- Creative direction evolves during production
- Asset delivery is unpredictable or ongoing
- "One more thing" additions are expected without compensation
- Approval is subjective and unlimited
- The contractor has no authority to declare "done"

### The Math of Unpaid Labor

Example: A layout project quoted at $500 for estimated 25 hours ($20/hr effective)

| Scenario | Actual Hours | Effective Rate |
|----------|--------------|----------------|
| Clean scope, locked assets | 25 | $20.00/hr |
| Minor scope creep (+20%) | 30 | $16.67/hr |
| Moderate revisions (+50%) | 37.5 | $13.33/hr |
| Active development (+100%) | 50 | $10.00/hr |
| Ongoing expansion (+200%) | 75 | $6.67/hr |

In practice, projects with active creative development routinely exceed estimates by 100-300%, pushing effective rates below minimum wage.

## Why Hourly is the Only Sustainable Model for Evolving Projects

### Hourly Compensation Aligns Incentives

- **Management pays for vision changes** - Additions cost money, forcing prioritization
- **Contractor is compensated for actual work** - No unpaid labor absorption
- **Timeline reflects reality** - Scope expansion visibly extends schedule
- **Both parties share risk** - Neither side bears disproportionate burden

### The Management Objection

> "Hourly doesn't incentivize efficiency"

This objection assumes the contractor controls scope. They do not. When management continuously expands requirements, efficiency is irrelevant—the work simply takes longer.

The real translation: "We want unlimited revisions without paying for them."

## The Accountability Gap

### What Management Must Own

For project-based freelance work to be viable as primary income:

1. **Lock scope before production** - Define exactly what "done" means
2. **Deliver assets on schedule** - Contractor cannot produce without inputs
3. **Limit revision rounds contractually** - Additional rounds = additional payment
4. **Separate creative development from production** - Finish writing before layout begins
5. **Communicate changes formally** - Scope changes require written amendments
6. **Accept that additions cost money** - No "quick favors" that consume hours

### The Reality Check

Most creative leadership in small publishers will not do this because:
- The vision is genuinely evolving (they don't know what they want yet)
- Formal process feels bureaucratic
- They assume contractors will absorb overflow as "part of the job"
- Budget was set before scope was understood

## Why This Creates "Side Gig" Economics

### The Sustainable Freelancer Myth

The fantasy: Talented freelancer builds client base, charges fair rates, works independently.

The reality:
- Clients expect fixed quotes for variable work
- Scope creep is normalized as "collaboration"
- Payment is delayed until "completion" (undefined)
- Contractor absorbs risk of client's own delays
- Effective hourly rate collapses below sustainability

### The Side Gig Trap

Because effective rates are unpredictable:
- Cannot rely on freelance as sole income
- Must maintain other employment for stability
- Freelance becomes "extra" work done evenings/weekends
- Burnout from working two jobs
- Quality suffers, reinforcing client perception of unreliability

### Who Survives Full-Time

The few who make freelance creative work their primary income typically:
- Have extremely clear contracts with scope limits
- Fire clients who scope creep without compensation
- Charge rates high enough to absorb some unpaid work
- Work with mature organizations that have locked processes
- Maintain 6+ months financial runway to weather gaps

Most do not have these conditions.

## The Path Forward

### For Freelancers

1. **Hourly or milestone with scope lock** - Never accept flat rate on evolving projects
2. **Document everything** - Emails confirming scope, change requests, delays
3. **Bill for all work** - Including "quick questions" and "small tweaks"
4. **Require asset delivery before deadlines** - Timeline doesn't start until inputs arrive
5. **Define "done" contractually** - Specific, objective completion criteria

### For Organizations

1. **Finish creative development before contracting production** - Lock the manuscript
2. **Budget for reality, not fantasy** - Scope changes cost money
3. **Pay hourly for exploratory work** - Fixed price only for fixed scope
4. **Own your own delays** - Contractor isn't responsible for your asset delivery
5. **Communicate formally** - Verbal additions don't exist

## Conclusion

Freelance creative work remains a side gig for most not because contractors lack skill or dedication, but because the industry normalizes transferring unlimited risk onto the people least able to absorb it.

Until management takes responsibility for defining and locking their own creative vision before production begins, contractors will continue to absorb unpaid labor, and sustainable freelance income will remain the exception rather than the rule.

---

*The solution is not "better freelancers." The solution is management that owns its vision before asking others to produce it.*
